## <i class="fa-regular fa-lightbulb"></i> What can we learn from this?

#### Expanding European surveillance beyond invasive infections

To estimate how extensive European countries perform surveillance in different culture materials we asked partner countries to estimate the population coverage and geographical representativeness of their national antimicrobial susceptibility (AST) data for pathogens isolated from four different culture materials.  


<i class="fa fa-handshake"></i> **Policy commitment:** Support EU Council Recommendation on AMR (B5a), ensuring that surveillance of AMR encompasses not only blood/CSF isolates but also all other isolates from clinical microbiology laboratories [condensed].

### Major trends:

- There is generally a high population coverage and geographical representativeness for pathogens isolated from blood/CSF. 
-	Coverage and representativeness drop for all pathogens when they are isolated from a culture material other than blood/CSF, such as urine.
-	Seven countries did not include AST data from culture materials other than blood/CSF in their national surveillance.


### Actions for change:

- Support countries to initiate national surveillance of priority pathogens from culture materials beyond invasive infections.

- Investigate the feasibility of European surveillance for additional culture materials.  Use the estimated population coverage to identify countries that have national AST data available as a starting point for further investigations.



### Why does expanding surveillance into more culture materials matter?

Monitoring AMR in less severe yet common infections, such as urinary tract infections, is vital for understanding both healthcare and community-wide AMR epidemiology and for guiding empiric treatment. These infections contribute significantly to antibiotic use and misuse. 
However, our baseline data show that surveillance gaps are common for non-invasive infections, restricting our ability to generate efficient data-driven treatment guidelines and the possibility of estimating the broader AMR burden. 
Improving surveillance data and deepening our understanding of resistance epidemiology in non-invasive infections are therefore essential for controlling AMR and supporting clinical decision-making. 
By beginning to expand European surveillance to include more culture materials, with the eventual goal of EARS-Net integration, a normative pressure would also be created, incentivising countries that currently lack such data to prioritise, mobilise, and strengthen their surveillance systems. This could mirror the progress achieved from the nearly three decades of European surveillance of blood/CSF isolates with EARS-Net that is likely one of the main factors behind the comprehensive surveillance in blood/CSF samples seen in our data.
